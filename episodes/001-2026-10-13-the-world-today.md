## 3. Narration script

### OPENING HOOK — 0:00–0:30

Riyadh's main international airport is closed. Missiles over the Saudi
capital. Oil above a hundred and six dollars a barrel. And in Ukraine, a
ceasefire announced by the American president is already coming apart.

This is The World in 10 — the world explained, one story at a time. Today is
Tuesday, October 13th, 2026. Here's what matters, and why.

### INTRODUCTION — 0:30–0:50

Welcome to The World in 10. This is your global briefing for Tuesday,
October 13th.

Today: the war in the Middle East widens as missiles strike Riyadh's
airport. Ukraine defies Washington over a fragile energy ceasefire. Markets
tumble across Asia as oil surges. The Nobel Prizes honor an economics duo
and a human rights jurist — and spark a diplomatic fight. Artificial
intelligence has its biggest week in months. And the clean-energy transition
gets an unlikely push — from war itself.

Let's begin.

### STORY 1 — MISSILES OVER RIYADH — 1:00–3:00

Our lead story: Saudi Arabia is under sustained missile and drone attack.

On Saturday, Iran-backed Houthi rebels struck King Khalid International
Airport in Riyadh — the kingdom's main air hub. At least thirteen people
were killed, including four Saudis and an American citizen, and more than
three hundred were wounded. Footage from inside the terminal showed
passengers diving for cover as part of the ceiling collapsed.

A second strike on Sunday killed one more person and injured eleven. And on
Monday, Saudi air defenses intercepted three more ballistic missiles — one
over Riyadh, two over the southwest of the country.

The airport remains closed until at least October 17th, with several
hundred flights cancelled. Britain's Foreign Office now advises against all
but essential travel to Riyadh, Jeddah, Dammam, and most Saudi airports. The
U.S. aviation authority has warned American airlines to exercise caution
over Saudi airspace.

Why it matters: this is the deadliest attack on Saudi Arabia in more than a
decade — and a dramatic widening of a war that began in February, when the
United States and Israel attacked Iran. The Houthis, armed and backed by
Tehran, have now shown they can reach deep into the kingdom and shut down
its busiest airport.

What next: Saudi Arabia's energy minister has vowed what he called an
equally serious response. President Trump says he's considering joining
Saudi retaliation against the Houthis in Yemen — a decision that would
deepen American involvement just three weeks before the U.S. midterm
elections. We'll go deeper on this story later in the episode.

### STORY 2 — UKRAINE'S FRAGILE ENERGY CEASEFIRE — 3:00–4:45

To Ukraine, where a ceasefire announced with great fanfare is already
fraying.

On Sunday, President Trump declared what he called an immediate "energy
ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine — a halt to attacks on power plants,
refineries, and gas facilities. Within hours, Ukraine's President Zelensky
said Russia had violated it by striking a Ukrainian gas facility.

And Zelensky went further: he vowed Ukraine would continue its own
retaliatory strikes against Russian oil refineries — openly defying an
order from the Trump administration to stop. Washington blames those
Ukrainian strikes for record American diesel prices. Zelensky's answer:
Kyiv will stop only if Moscow stops first. Analysts say that's unlikely,
with winter approaching and Russia trying to cripple Ukraine's heating and
electricity.

Meanwhile, Trump announced a separate, contentious deal: Russia would
supply millions of tons of diesel to the United States over the next three
months — in defiance, critics say, of sanctions Trump himself signed weeks
ago. Seven U.S. senators, from both parties, are demanding he reverse it.
The volumes involved, analysts note, equal roughly twelve hours of global
diesel consumption — barely a dent in prices.

Why it matters: with American midterm elections on November 3rd, fuel
prices are political dynamite. But the episode reveals the limits of
deal-making by announcement: a ceasefire with no agreed start date, no
verification mechanism, and no sign Moscow has even responded.

What next: watch whether Russia formally answers the ceasefire proposal —
and whether Ukraine's refinery campaign continues into winter.

### STORY 3 — MARKETS SELLOFF AS OIL JUMPS — 4:45–6:15

Markets are feeling all of this. It was a risk-off day across Asia.

South Korea's KOSPI plunged six percent. Japan's Nikkei fell two-point-six
percent. The broad Asia-Pacific index dropped two percent, led by
technology shares. On Wall Street overnight, the Nasdaq slipped half a
percent as chipmakers sold off, while the Dow managed a small gain.

The through-line is energy and geopolitics. Brent crude is near one hundred
and six dollars a barrel, close to a two-week high, as the Riyadh strikes
and the fragile Ukraine truce keep supply risks front and center. The U.S.
dollar sits near an eighteen-month high. Gold eased; Bitcoin slipped below
eighty-three thousand.

Why it matters: as one strategist put it, markets are rediscovering that
geopolitics matters most when it feeds back into inflation. With oil
elevated, pressure stays on interest rates — which is exactly what
expensive growth and AI stocks don't want to hear.

What next: a packed calendar. Big U.S. bank earnings begin tomorrow,
American inflation data lands Wednesday, and Federal Reserve Chair Kevin
Warsh speaks Thursday in Bangkok. Finance ministers from 191 countries are
meeting all week in Thailand for the IMF and World Bank annual meetings.

### STORY 4 — THE NOBEL PRIZES — 6:15–7:45

Now to Stockholm and Oslo, where this year's Nobel Prizes are set — and one
of them has ignited a diplomatic firestorm.

The Economics Prize went to two Americans: Robert Hall of Stanford and
Michael Woodford of Columbia, honored for work on the microeconomic
foundations of macroeconomic stabilization — in plain terms, models that
help central banks and governments cushion economies against shocks like
energy crises. Their research underpins tools used worldwide.

The Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay, the 85-year-old South African jurist
and former UN human rights chief, recognized for her efforts to promote
peace and international law. Pillay served as a judge on the International
Criminal Court and led the UN inquiry into the occupied Palestinian
territory.

Hours after the announcement, the Trump administration imposed sweeping
financial sanctions on the International Criminal Court itself — a move the
court condemned as an assault on the rule of law, and which eight allied
foreign ministers, from Canada to Japan, publicly opposed. President Trump,
who has openly campaigned for the prize, called the decision disgraceful.

Why it matters: beyond the personalities, the prize is a statement about
the rules-based order at a moment when it's under severe strain — from
sanctions on courts to missiles on airports.

What next: the prizes will be formally awarded in December.

### STORY 5 — AI'S BIG WEEK — 7:45–9:30

Artificial intelligence had one of its biggest weeks in months — for
breakthroughs, warnings, and politics alike.

Meta released two new frontier models, Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full,
which it calls its most capable systems to date — aimed at expert work in
law, finance, health, and coding, and positioned as rivals to Anthropic and
OpenAI.

But the industry's mood was captured better in Singapore, where the CEOs of
Broadcom and Intel warned that money — not chips, not electricity — is now
the biggest constraint on the AI buildout. Broadcom's Hock Tan said
financing could become a bigger obstacle than any hardware shortage, and
compared some AI investors to casino gamblers. Intel's Lip-Bu Tan questioned
the return on investment. Chip stocks fell for a fourth straight session.

Then came the regulation debate. Microsoft's president, Brad Smith, told
the BBC that AI now faces less regulatory oversight than washing machines
or milk cartons — and called for emergency brakes, mandatory incident
reporting, and independent safety vetting. Days earlier, Microsoft's CEO
made a similar call.

And in Washington, President Trump tapped his national intelligence
director, Jay Clayton, to lead a new federal AI task force — the "Super
Intelligence Force" — while the Federal Trade Commission is reported to be
investigating OpenAI and Anthropic over consumer risks.

Why it matters: the AI boom is entering a more skeptical phase. The
questions are no longer just what the technology can do, but who pays for
it, who governs it, and what happens when it goes wrong.

What next: watch whether Meta's models shift the competitive balance — and
whether any real regulation follows the rhetoric.

### STORY 6 — WAR'S CLIMATE SIDE-EFFECT — 9:30–10:30

And a striking paradox to close the briefing: the Iran war may be doing
more for the climate than years of summits.

Back in January, the International Energy Agency expected global oil demand
to grow by nearly a million barrels a day this year. By September, it was
forecasting a decline of two and a half million barrels a day instead —
driven by the impasse between the U.S. and Iran.

Less oil means less pollution. Global fossil-fuel emissions are on track to
fall around half a percent in 2026, according to Carbon Brief — the first
decline since the Covid pandemic in 2020. Electric vehicle sales rose four
percent in the second quarter, as high fuel prices pushed buyers toward
alternatives — in Europe, EV sales hit record highs.

Why it matters: price shocks change behavior faster than pledges. But
analysts caution that driving less is temporary — what lasts is what people
buy.

What next: next month's COP31 climate summit in Turkey, where the EU will
push to turn national climate pledges into binding action after the world
officially missed the Paris goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees.

### THE DEEPER CONTEXT — 10:30–12:30

Now, the deeper story: how did missiles reach Riyadh? To understand this
weekend, you have to go back to February.

That's when the United States and Israel launched a war against Iran — a
conflict that sent oil above one hundred dollars and strangled the Strait
of Hormuz, the waterway carrying roughly a fifth of the world's oil and
gas. Iran's leverage was geography: close the strait, and the global
economy chokes.

Eight months later, the war has metastasized. The Houthis — the Iran-backed
movement that controls much of Yemen, including Red Sea coastline — have
become Tehran's longest arm. They've attacked shipping, and now they've
demonstrated something new: the ability to strike Riyadh's international
airport repeatedly, killing at least twenty people over the past week and
forcing its closure.

Consider what that means. Saudi Arabia is the world's swing oil producer
and America's key Gulf ally. Its airports are now targets. Airlines are
suspending flights. The UK is telling its citizens to stay away from
Riyadh. At least ten massive oil tankers have stopped or reversed course
near Fujairah, threatening the workaround routes that had restored Gulf
exports.

Riyadh's response tells you how seriously it takes this. Saudi Arabia plans
to expand pipeline capacity bypassing the Strait of Hormuz by forty
percent — to ten million barrels a day within two to three years. That's a
multi-year, strategic rewiring of the world's oil map, designed for a world
where Hormuz can never be fully trusted again.

And then there's Washington's dilemma. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman
expects American military help — Saudi Arabia has bought billions in U.S.
arms. But President Trump knows that deeper entanglement, three weeks
before the midterms, could be politically toxic. American voters are already
paying for this war at the diesel pump.

Three scenarios from here. One: diplomacy somehow freezes the escalation —
unlikely, given Tehran calls Washington's latest Hormuz proposals
"inadequate." Two: a grinding stalemate of intermittent strikes, with
airports and shipping under rolling threat. Three: full escalation, with
the U.S. joining Saudi strikes on Yemen — and Iran answering against Gulf
neighbors.

The through-line: this is no longer a war contained to Iran. It's a
regional conflict redrawing energy routes, airline maps, and alliances in
real time.

### WHAT TO WATCH NEXT — 12:30–14:00

What to watch next.

Tomorrow: America's biggest banks report earnings — the unofficial start of
earnings season, and a health check on the consumer.

Wednesday: U.S. inflation data. With oil above one-oh-six and diesel at
record highs, this number matters for the Federal Reserve — and for the
midterms.

Thursday: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh speaks in Bangkok, on the sidelines of the
IMF and World Bank meetings, where 191 countries are debating AI, debt, and
geopolitical upheaval.

Next month: the COP31 climate summit in Turkey, where the EU will push to
turn climate pledges into binding action.

And November 3rd: America's midterm elections — with diesel prices, the
Iran war, and now Riyadh all on the ballot in one way or another.

### CLOSING — 14:00–15:00

The takeaway: the world's crises are no longer distant headlines. A missile
over Riyadh moves the price at your gas station. A ceasefire announced on
social media unravels by morning. And the institutions built to keep the
peace — courts, treaties, central banks — are being tested all at once.

That's the world today. If this briefing helped you understand it,
subscribe to The World in 10 — the world explained, one story at a time.
We'll see you tomorrow at nine.
